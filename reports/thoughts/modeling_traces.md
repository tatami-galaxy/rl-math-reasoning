### Modeling Reasoning Traces

Language models post-trained for reasoning tasks (math, code) are trained to produce long "chains of thoughts" (COT) before producing the final output [[Wei et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903); [DeepSeek-R1, 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.12948)].  The consensus behind why this enhances model performance is that the number of serial computations a transformer based LM can perform is bounded by constant model depth [[Merrill & Sabharwal, 2022](https://direct.mit.edu/tacl/article/doi/10.1162/tacl_a_00493/112604/Saturated-Transformers-are-Constant-Depth); [Feng et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12875)]. COT allows a constant-depth transformer to tackle problems that require sequential computation, by unrolling the computation across time-steps [[Feng et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12875); [Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07923)]. This is also supported by evidence that more complex problems typically require longer COTs [[Wu et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.07266v1); [Less is More Tokens, 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.05226)], though some work cautions that trace length may reflect distributional distance from training data rather than purely computational complexity [[Chiang et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.07339)]

What is the optimal COT given a prompt or a set of prompts from a dataset? If we had access to this, then we could attempt to train models to emit this optimal COT. We cannot expect to have such access. However if we restrict ourselves to tasks which frontier models are able to reasonably solve, then we can obtain COTs that lead to correct outputs. Can we utilize these gold COTs? We want to utilize them in the context of training new, small language models. Current training pipelines run SFT on these traces to teach models how to reason with COTs. This is desirably simple and scalable, and leads to measurable performance gains [[DeepSeek-R1, 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.12948); [Muennighoff et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.19393); [Skill-Aware Distillation, 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.10109)]. This also implies that the traces contain useful properties are transferable across models of different scales and architectures. However is SFT on the traces the optimal thing to do?

Assuming the model primarily uses COT as a mechanism to perform sequential computation, how efficient is this computation? That is, how efficently does the model utilize its COT token budget? Given a prompt and a generated COT, is there a more concise COT that allows the same model to perform the same computation? Recent research suggests that even for a single prompt, COT lengths can have large variances, suggesting that models often produce sub-optimally long COTs [[Ding et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16419); [Wang et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04697); [Yeo et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.13417)]. This should also be expected to occur more frequently during early post-training, and indeed  DeepSeek-R1 documents that models in early RL-based post-training produce redundant and poorly structured traces that improve over further training [[DeepSeek-R1, 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.12948)]. Bai et al. (2025) find that for models like R1 and QwQ, extending CoT length can also hurt performance due to the model reaching a correct intermediate answer and then deviating. [[Bai et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12215v1)]. Therefore we can conclude that even gold COTs can potentially have redundancies and training models to reproduce them verbatim might be sub-optimal. It also teaches models to produce similarly long traces with redundancies, hurting efficiency. 

What about the semantics of the traces? As mentioned before, the consensus is that models primarily use COTs as scratchpads for computation and there is no constraint on the model to produce traces that faithfully represent underlying computation [[Turpin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388); [Lanham et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13702)]. Turpin et al. show that models change their answers in response to biasing features without ever mentioning those features in their CoT, while Lanham et al. find that larger models tend to produce *less* faithful reasoning on most tasks. However we posit that while the semantics of the traces are not tightly coupled with underlying computation, the semantics do matter - perhaps not in a simple human interpretable way. This is a reasonable position since otherwise, training models on gold COTs would not be different from training them on arbitrary, noisy COTs [[Ma et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.04667v1)]. SFT on pruned or corrupted gold COTs also lead to performance degradations [[D-COT, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21786); [Fragile Thoughts, 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.03332)]. Therefore the sematics of the traces should contain meaningful information regarding the underlying computation, but the mapping between them might not be the obvious natual langauge interpretation.

Lets summarize our intuitions about COTs generated from frontier models. While often leading to correct conclusions, they can be sub-optimally long and their semantics might not accurately reflect underlying computations. They do contain *some* useful signals since doing SFT on them helps new models learn how to reason.

*Can we try to model these traces in a way that captures the useful signals they contain? If so, can we then use such a model to efficiently teach new models how to reason?*

These are two distinct objectives, but the second objective puts additional contraints on the first objective. Not only do we want to model *useful* properties of the traces but it has to be in an actionable way. We can think about how to jointly satisfy these two objectives, but lets start with the first objective and see if we can make any progress.

### Idea 1 : Learn a vector field

One way we can try to model the traces is by treating them as trajectories through some vector field. Each token or segment (design choice) of the COT can be thought of as a point in a trajectory that culminates in the final generated solution. Can we attempt to learn a vector field the governs these trajectories for a class of prompts?

Why vector fields? We could also model traces with many other generative models. But vector fields have many properties that we have deep insights about and these can be potentially leveraged for our applications (the second objective) : 

- Divergence : Regions of positive divergence are where trajectories drift apart which might imply semantic diversification. Texts passing through this region are becoming more dissimilar over time. This might correspond to a topic branching point where an initially coherent theme splits into subtopics. Regions of negative divergence are sinks. This could indicate semantic consolidation, where diverse texts are funneling toward a common meaning or topic.

- Velocity : Regions where velocity mnagnitude is large are where text embeddings change rapidly which might correspond to fast semantic transitions. 

- Helmholtz Decomposition :  Any sufficiently smooth vector field can be decomposed into a combination of Φ, a scalar potential (irrotational/gradient part) and A, a vector potential (solenoidal/rotational part). This decomposition tells us how much of the dynamics is gradient-like (flowing downhill toward attractors) versus rotational (cycling). For text embeddings, a strongly gradient-dominated field would suggest a landscape where texts evolve toward fixed semantic attractors, while a rotational component would indicate cyclic or oscillatory semantic dynamics, that is topics that revisit or orbit around themes.

- Other interesting properties : Spectral Properties of the Jacobian, Laplacian, Curl etc. 

We can alo experiment with different learnable latent spaces to embed the traces into, so that it captures the *right* information content. The numerous computable properties of a vector field also gives us indications on how we can potentially leverage the field to control COTs of a model being trained. 

### Neural / Latent ODE

A neural or latent ODE parameterizes the vector field as a neural network f(x, t; θ), where dx/dt = f(x, t; θ), then integrates it to produce trajectories. Neural ODEs learn f by backpropagating through an ODE solver using the adjoint method. Each sequence becomes an initial value problem.
Latent ODEs encode sequences into a latent space first, then learn the ODE there. They also handle irregular/noisily sampled data which COTs are akin to. We can start with this and later maybe also consider formulations like stochastic interpolants.
